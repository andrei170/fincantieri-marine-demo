How to spec an LNG bunker barge.

A working reference for midstream operators and marine fuel companies preparing to commission an LNG bunker barge build. Five specs that actually move a quote, the procurement traps that cost six figures, and the questions a Great Lakes shipyard asks before pricing.

If you are quoting an LNG bunker barge build, the answer to the first five questions a shipyard asks already determines half the price. Most operators answer the wrong five.

LNG as a marine fuel is no longer the experimental story it was a decade ago. Bunkering operations have moved out of the engineering pilot phase and into routine commercial supply. The number of LNG-fueled deepsea vessels in service crossed a threshold where dedicated bunker barges, not portable ISO tanks or shore-side flange transfers, became the operationally rational answer for most major ports.

That shift made the LNG bunker barge a real procurement category. It also exposed how few buyers, even sophisticated midstream operators, know how to specify one. The result is the same pattern repeating across quotes from three different yards: a build sheet that prices a 5,000 m³ barge against a 3,000 m³ barge against a 7,500 m³ barge, all answering the same RFP, because the operator did not lock the inputs that drive cost.

This brief is the operator-side version of the conversation a shipyard wants to have. Five specs that move the quote. The traps that compound. And the questions to walk in with.

01 The shift toward dedicated bunker barges

Three factors pushed LNG bunkering off the dock and onto the water.

First, deepsea vessel growth. The order book for LNG-dual-fuel containerships, car carriers, and tankers compounded faster than shore-side bunkering infrastructure could keep up. Operators needed a way to bunker without occupying a berth, and the bunker barge became the answer the same way it has been for residual fuel oil for half a century.

Second, schedule density. A containership bunkering shore-side ties up a berth for hours that the port operator would rather sell to cargo. Ship-to-ship LNG transfer puts the bunkering operation on a separate vessel, in parallel with cargo ops, and recovers the berth.

Third, regulatory specificity. SIGTTO, ISO, and ABS guidance on marine LNG transfer matured to the point where a purpose-built barge is no longer an experimental asset. It is a classed vessel with a known regulatory envelope.

The buyer's job is to translate that envelope into a hull, a tank arrangement, and a transfer system that fits a specific port and a specific charter customer. That is where most procurement processes go sideways.

02 Capacity, and the mistake of optimising for the headline number

The first number every buyer wants on the spec sheet is total LNG capacity. It is also the number that is least useful in isolation.

What actually matters is delivered capacity per bunkering operation, which is a function of: total tank volume, the deadband between minimum and maximum operating fill levels, the boil-off allowance, and the time available within the receiving vessel's bunker window. A 6,500 m³ barge that can only deliver 4,800 m³ in a 4-hour window is operationally smaller than a 5,500 m³ barge that delivers 4,800 m³ in 3 hours.

Specify the operation, not the headline. Three questions to lock first:

  • Receiving vessel size envelope. Largest expected receiver, in cubic metres of LNG demand. Plan to the 90th percentile, not the median.
  • Bunker window. The realistic time-on-station the receiving customer will allow. Most charter agreements clamp this between 4 and 8 hours.
  • Operating cycle. Number of bunker ops per week the barge is expected to support, including reload cycles at the source terminal.

03 Hull form: ATB, ITB, or self-propelled

Three hull architectures dominate the current order book.

Articulated tug barge (ATB). A dedicated tug locks into a notch on the barge's stern via a coupling system. Cheaper to build and crew than a self-propelled barge. Faster to redeploy: same tug can service multiple barges. The dominant choice for inland and coastal LNG bunkering routes.

Integrated tug barge (ITB). Permanent, structurally rigid coupling. Operates as one unit at sea, separates only for major maintenance. Stronger seakeeping than ATB, marginally more expensive build, lower operational flexibility.

Self-propelled bunker barge. Dedicated propulsion, often diesel-electric or LNG-dual-fuel itself. Higher build cost, higher crew cost, but no notch interface to maintain. Worth it for high-utilisation routes.

The choice cascades into hull length, beam, and propulsion. It also cascades into shipyard selection. A Great Lakes commercial shipyard with multiple ATB hulls in its history will quote an ATB build differently than a Gulf Coast yard quoting one for the first time.

04 Propulsion (or, when "propulsion" really means "tug spec")

For ATB configurations, the propulsion conversation is split: the tug carries the engines, the barge carries the cargo. The buyer is specifying two assets, not one. The temptation to under-spec the tug is the single most common, and most expensive, procurement mistake in this category.

A tug under-rated for the loaded barge in coupled operation will cost the operator either in transit time, in fuel, or in coupling-system wear. All three compound. Re-engining a tug five years into service to recover bunker-window margins is roughly as expensive as the original tug.

For self-propelled hulls, the live conversation is dual-fuel versus diesel-only. Operating a diesel-only LNG bunker barge in 2026 reads, to charterers and to port authorities, as inconsistent. Dual-fuel adds capital cost and saves it back in three to five years through reduced compliance friction and a cleaner sustainability narrative for charter customers.

05 Manifold and transfer system

The manifold is where most cost surprises live. SIGTTO compliance, ESD-1 and ESD-2 emergency shutdown integration, dry-disconnect couplings, vapour return management, and bunker meter calibration are all real engineering line items that the headline-capacity-number conversation tends to gloss over.

Three things to specify early:

  • Hose handling. Reel-mounted, crane-deployed, or composite. Drives weather window and crew workload.
  • Vapour return. Line size and isolation. Drives bunker rate at high-fill conditions.
  • Bunker metering. Coriolis vs ultrasonic. Drives invoice accuracy and dispute frequency. Charter customers care.

06 Classification, flag, and what the yard actually builds to

For a US-flag bunker barge serving US ports, the working envelope is ABS class, USCG Subchapter D for tank vessels, IGC Code for the cargo containment system, and Jones Act compliance for the build. The yard's experience inside that exact envelope is worth more than its experience inside a comparable European or Asian regulatory frame.

A US shipyard that has delivered ATB hulls and LNG-capable tank vessels under ABS class and USCG inspection is operating in a regulatory groove the design will fit. A yard quoting outside that groove will price it as bespoke. The price difference is rarely small.

07 The procurement traps that compound

Three patterns recur often enough to be procurement-side red flags:

  1. Optimising the barge against the median customer instead of the worst-case customer. Charter renewals are won on the largest receiving vessel the barge can handle, not the average one.
  2. Treating the tug as an afterthought. See Section 04. Re-engining costs roughly equal a new tug.
  3. Skipping the operating-cycle interview. The barge that perfectly fits one route is often the wrong barge for the route the operator actually charters six months later. Build the spec around a flexible operating envelope, not a single trade.
The cheapest barge to procure is the barge that fits the second charter. The most expensive is the one perfectly built for the first.

The follow-on conversation a shipyard wants to have is not about price. It is about charter context, port pair flexibility, and the operating envelope the barge needs to survive the first two charter renewals. A yard quoting without that context is quoting a hull, not a vessel.
